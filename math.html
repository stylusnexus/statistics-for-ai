Math for AI: Linear Algebra & Calculus Essentials

The vectors, matrices, derivatives and integrals that AI and machine learning actually use — each one tied to something a model does, not a textbook exercise.

7 lessons Sequential — each builds on the last High-school algebra is enough Quizzes included

Linear algebra

  1. Vectors: Embeddings, Dot Products & Measuring Similarity

    A vector is a list of numbers that can be a point, an arrow, or a meaning. Add them, measure them, take dot products, and learn why dot product, cosine, and distance pick different winners in retrieval.

  2. Matrices: Transformations, Layers & Why Shapes Matter

    A neural-network layer, an attention score table and a batch of embeddings are all one object: a grid of numbers that turns vectors into vectors. Learn to multiply them by hand and to read shapes before your code crashes.

  3. Eigenvectors, SVD & Low-Rank Structure: PCA and LoRA

    Eigenvectors, the singular value decomposition, and low-rank approximation, worked by hand on small matrices. Then the two places AI uses them most: PCA for finding the main axis of data and LoRA for fine-tuning with a fraction of the parameters.

Calculus

  1. Derivatives: Slope, Rate of Change & the Functions ML Uses

    Slope, the limit of shrinking gaps, tangent lines, and the handful of rules that differentiate everything a network uses: squared error, sigmoid, ReLU, log loss. Read a derivative's sign to know which way to move, and check it numerically.

  2. Partial Derivatives & Gradients: Which Way Is Downhill?

    A loss with millions of weights is a landscape, and the gradient is the arrow that points uphill. Compute partial derivatives by hand, run three steps of gradient descent, and see why a learning rate that is too large blows up.

  3. The Chain Rule & Backpropagation: How Gradients Flow Through a Network

    A network is a long chain of small functions, and the chain rule is how the loss gets traced back to every weight. Work a complete backprop by hand on a tiny network, see why gradients vanish or explode, and learn what your framework is really doing.

  4. Integrals: Accumulation, Area & the Continuous Side of Probability

    The last piece of calculus: integrals as accumulated area, the fundamental theorem, and why every density, expected value and softmax denominator is an integral. Includes a Monte Carlo estimate whose error shrinks like 1/√n.
